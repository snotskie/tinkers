# Mariah’s Tinkers for Legal and Academic Writing 📑

Choose a prompt. Choose an approach. Get away from the keyboard. Listen to your gut and *wriiiiiiiiite*.

Can’t choose? [Roll two dice](https://www.google.com/search?q=2d6).

## Tinker Prompts

1. Why does clarity morally matter?  
2. What were your school lunches like?  
3. If you could magically rearrange your kitchen however you wanted, how would you rearrange it?  
4. Tell me everything about the building across from you.  
5. What is (or isn’t) the difference between knowing something vs. having experience with something?  
6. What would you most like to attempt if you were allowed to fail?

## Tinker Approaches

### 1\. Mine your Intuition

Split the task into Jotting and Memoing.

Use scratch paper to jot ideas, phrases, imagery, examples, memories, and so on as you fully mine your intuition about the prompt. Whatever comes up, jot it down. Then, keep mining further. When you think there’s no gas left in the tank, keep mining further. Get up and go for a walk: If a little sun on your skin brings up new ideas, keep mining further.

Then, start to string your jotted notes together by drawing lines showing the path of the “story.” Group related things up. Cut some things.

Once the story lives full and clear in your head, grab a new sheet and start writing the memo. Full sentences, clear enough that others could understand them, but don’t get hung up on perfection.

### 2\. Break it Down to Build it Up

Write your response to the prompt one sentence at a time, writing each sentence on a separate index card.

If you are writing an *argument*, then think about your index cards like this: One card is your conclusion, and all the rest are your premises. Assume all of your premise cards are true. Is there any way you can possibly imagine for your conclusion to still be false? If so, keep adding or revising your premise cards until there are no more holes you can poke in your argument.

If you are writing a *description*, then think about your index cards like this: Assume I’ve read all your cards, I understand them, and I believe them. Is there any way you can possibly imagine that I might find your account incomplete? If so, keep adding or revising your cards until you’re giving a full and fair account. Don’t only think about getting the facts straight (do get the facts straight), but also think about the feelings of the human experiences involved. Don’t only think about expressing the feelings either, but also unpack them by asking repeatedly, “if this feeling had a voice what would it say” and “what does this feeling tell me is at stake in this situation?”

Either way, once you have all your cards written out, arrange them on the floor. Group up similar things. See how ideas flow. Consider putting things in a “wrong” order to see how it feels. Keep experimenting with different ways to arrange your ideas until you’ve found one that both surprises you and “works” to answer the tinker prompt.

### 3\. Read with a Pen in your Hand

Stand on the shoulders of giants. Jot down at the top of a scratch sheet of paper your gut instinct for what your answer to the prompt is—just enough to get you in a ballpark.

Then find a reputable journal that might help you flesh out your response by [browsing SJR](https://www.scimagojr.com/journalrank.php). Tip: Use the Subject Categories dropdown to narrow results to, for example, Philosophy. Click around a few journals, see if their “Scope” seems to overlap the ballpark you’re interested in, and visit their “Home” pages. See how easy their homepages are to navigate and search. Keep notes on your scratch paper about journals that seem useful (or not).

Once you’ve found a promising journal, find a promising article that you can access. Print out the article and read it with a pen in your hand. Write notes in the paper margins about four things: (1) Information about who the author is and the perspective they are coming from. (2) Information about who their intended audience is and what they appear to assume their audience already knows. (3) What the author claims the point of their article is, their findings, and their conclusions. (4) Anything in their paper that is actually useful for you for answering the Tinker prompt. Actually write the number in the margin and state what you see in your own words in a very short, but complete, sentence. For example, “(2) Their audience is K-12 teachers.” If multiple numbers apply, list them both, like “(3/4).”

Finally, transfer all your “4s” to a fresh sheet and clean them up into a coherent (likely partial) answer to the Tinker prompt. Don’t quote. Practice breaking a complex idea into multiple sentences. Practice citing. How you cite is up to you, so long as you pick a consistent and clear citation format. Practice not “leading the nose”: That is, do NOT say "Geistfeld argues that such and such (2017)." This moves the active verb of the statement from the point you are trying to make to "argues,” which makes it easy to half-say an idea. The point of the sentence isn't that some guy made an argument. Just say your point (your “4”), cite, and move on. I recommend using "*id*" as shorthand for "same as the previous cite." For example:

The more an autonomous vehicle drives, the more data it collects, and conceivably the safer it will be (Geistfeld 2017). But we can also pool the miles of vehicles in a "fleet" of like model and operating system, since their data can be shared between them (*id*.). Federal agencies could then set minimum requirements for the number of pre-market miles driven by a manufacturer's fleet (*id*.). Federal rules may also need to specify optimal safety standards for autonomous vehicles (*id*.). If federal standards are not optimal, but instead minimal, then state-level safety regulations may pre-empt the federal-level standards, which will cause greater legal uncertainty for manufacturers and insurers (*id*.). This uncertainty could delay deployment for technologies that have promise to improve public safety.

This breaks down Geistfeld's complex argument, it makes it clear what ideas are from that source, and it makes it clear that the last sentence is from me.

### 4\. Write within Constraints

Come up with an artificial constraint for your response to the prompt and jot it at the top of a scratch sheet of paper. For example: Answer in exactly 5 sentences. Answer using only monosyllabic words. Answer without using the letter “e.” And so on. Constraints can make creativity *easier* and they force you to stretch writing muscles you haven’t stretched in a while.

Then, write your response to the prompt, following the constraint you set. Write in ink. Instead of erasing, lightly cross out. That way you can see your revisions along the way. It’s okay to have 90% of the page filled with “mistakes” or “bad ideas” or “trash” that you’ve crossed out. That’s part of the process\! Transfer only the “clean” response to a fresh sheet once you’re done, keeping the draft one around so you can see your own process.

### 5\. Memo where your Mouth Is

Split the task into Jotting and Memoing.

Find an actual person (friend, family member, peer) and have a real conversation with them about the prompt. Not a formal interview—just talk. Don’t monopolize the conversation yourself, and don’t only listen passively to what they say.

As you talk, take notes as jottings on a scratch sheet of paper. Don’t try to capture everything. Jot down just enough for you to recall interesting things later. Consider: What questions do they ask? Where do they push back? What do they assume? What story do they tell when responding to the prompt? Where does the conversation force you to clarify what you actually think?

As soon as you can afterward, refer to your jottings and write out your answer to the tinker prompt as a memo, informed by that real dialogue. Full sentences, clear enough that others could understand them, but don’t get hung up on perfection. Give an account of the conversation, answer the prompt, and marry these two aims together.

### 6\. Trace the Journey

Write three brief versions of your answer to the prompt from three different perspectives, one or two sentences each, each answer on a different index card:

* Past you: How would you have answered this five years ago?  
* Present you: How do you answer it now?  
* Future you: How do you imagine you might answer it in ten years?

Then, add an index card between the Past and Present cards. On it, pinpoint and write down what it was that changed how you answer the question. Try to get as precise as you can. At one point you believed X, now you believe Y, what happened in the middle?

Then track down evidence to back that “what changed” index card up. Some places you might look: Old texts, diaries, emails, past school assignments, asking a few different people who’ve known you for a while, photos on your phone, paper documents, etc. The point isn’t to find “proof” but to find a range of things that “crystallize” together to tell the same story, outside just relying on your own memory. Jot down the evidence you find onto new index cards and place them around the “what changed” card.

Then imagine what events the next ten years might hold for you. Jot down one event per card and place these cards between the Past and Present cards.

Finally, write on a new scratch sheet of paper the story you see unfolding in front of you in these index cards, ie., the story of you (or, better said, *this* story of you—we’re complex things\!)
